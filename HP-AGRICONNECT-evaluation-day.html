      How a satellite sees a field

      Sentinel-2 as the example

      A satellite never sees "wheat". It measures how much sunlight each pixel reflects in a set of colour bands. Crops differ in that pattern and in how it changes through the season, and the models read both.

      1 · LIGHT

      Sun hits the canopy

      Leaves absorb blue and red for photosynthesis and bounce back near-infrared strongly. Water in leaves absorbs short-wave infrared.

      2 · SENSOR

      13 bands recorded

      Blue, green, red and NIR at 10 m. Red-edge and SWIR at 20 m. One image every few days.

      3 · DOWNLINK

      Level-1C

      Ground stations receive it. It's geo-corrected to top-of-atmosphere reflectance.

      4 · CLEAN

      Level-2A

      Atmospheric correction gives surface reflectance. A per-pixel mask removes cloud, shadow and snow.

      5 · HILLS

      Terrain correction

      A DEM evens out sunlit and shaded slopes. Pixels are clipped to each Plot ID's boundary.

      6 · INSIGHT

      Indices to models

      NDVI, EVI and the rest per plot, per date. The season-long curve feeds crop, health and harvest models.

      Radar runs alongside. Sentinel-1 sends its own microwave pulse and measures the echo, called backscatter, in decibels. It sees through monsoon cloud and at night. The VH echo grows with crop volume and stays high over woody orchards all year.

      Slope and shade: raw values vs indices
      A wheat field in March
      
      
      On a slope facing away from the sun, every band gets darker. Raw reflectance changes a lot, but a ratio like NDVI barely moves. Terrain correction with a DEM removes what's left.
